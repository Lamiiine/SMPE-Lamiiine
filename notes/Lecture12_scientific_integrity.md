# Lecture12: Data Integrity & P-Hacking

Data integrity is about making sure research results reflect reality, not bias, manipulation, or selective reporting. The biggest threats often come from incentives to publish positive or exciting findings.

Publication bias hides negative results, which distorts scientific knowledge and can cause real world harm, as seen in cases where flawed research influenced public health decisions.

Researchers can unintentionally or deliberately mislead through practices like changing hypotheses after seeing data, manipulating data or images, or repeatedly testing until something looks statistically significant.

P hacking happens when researchers tweak analysis choices until results pass significance thresholds, creating false discoveries that look real but are not reproducible.

Modern risks include AI generated fake data and images, making verification harder and increasing the need for strong detection methods.

The main defenses are transparency and pre planning: pre registering studies, reporting all analyses, correcting for multiple testing, sharing data and code, and clearly separating exploratory from confirmatory research.

Plagiarism and authorship abuse also damage integrity, and all listed authors are responsible for the work’s validity.

Misconduct is detected through community review, replication, software tools, and investigation processes, but prevention is better through good documentation, data archiving, and ethical training.

Warning signs of unreliable research include suspiciously perfect statistics, missing raw data, overly clean datasets, or unclear methods.

Main takeaway:
Science depends on trust, and maintaining data integrity requires careful design, honest analysis, transparent reporting, and constant scrutiny.
