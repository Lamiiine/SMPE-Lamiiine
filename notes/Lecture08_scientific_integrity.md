# Lecture08: Publishing & Integrity

Scientific publishing exists to share knowledge, verify results, and build cumulative research, while also establishing who discovered something first.

Research papers follow a standard structure (abstract → intro → methods → results → discussion → references) to make studies easy to evaluate and replicate.

Peer review is the main quality control: experts evaluate validity, methods, and contribution before publication. It improves reliability but can be slow, biased, and inconsistent.

Researchers can publish through journals, conferences, or preprints. Preprints allow fast sharing and priority claims before formal review.

Authorship should reflect real contributions (tracked via roles like CRediT), but unethical practices exist (ghost, gift, or paid authorship).

Research impact is measured using metrics like Impact Factor (journals) and h-index (researchers), but these can be misleading and are often gamed.

Open Access aims to make research freely available, using different funding models (author pays, self-archive, or free OA journals).

Papers may be retracted if fraud, plagiarism, or major errors are found, usually marked but not deleted.

A major risk in academia is optimizing for metrics instead of quality (Goodhart’s Law), leading to bad practices like p-hacking or splitting results into many small papers.

Main takeaway:
The goal of publishing is trustworthy knowledge sharing,  not just producing papers or chasing metrics.
